# Arrival-supplement results

The externally scheduled experiment shows a clear arrival-load effect and mixed dispatcher effects. Increasing the offered-load fraction from 0.50 to 0.85 raised mean response in all 16 state/pattern comparisons for each arm. At fixed offered load, bursts of eight raised mean response relative to constant arrivals in 14 of 16 comparisons for each arm. Neither the online dispatcher nor the burst penalty was uniformly better or worse across the recorded cases. These are descriptive comparisons of the completed bounded experiment, with no new confidence intervals, significance tests, or acceptance decision.

All eight native calibrations and all 128 evaluation trials completed in allocation 4871240 on kh083. The validated production summary covers 32 cells, each with two technical repetitions and two arms. Calibrated native capacity was 1,119.965–1,204.456 layer jobs/s for B=128 and 991.846–1,010.200 for B=1,024. This capacity belongs to the new synchronized command/ACK wrapper. It is not the throughput of the earlier back-to-back host submission path (native EPLB remained synchronous) or a full language-model server.

The strongest consistent response pattern for the online dispatcher is the B=1,024 burst workload: all four cells improve at each offered load. Their mean response changes from 4.340 to 4.136 ms at ρ=0.50 and from 6.222 to 5.248 ms at ρ=0.85. The constant-arrival results prevent a broad latency claim: at B=1,024 and ρ=0.85, the corresponding descriptive average increases from 2.770 to 3.655 ms, with only one of four cells improving. At B=128, three of four cells improve under high-load bursts while the across-cell mean worsens from 4.855 to 5.088 ms. Direction counts and effect magnitudes therefore answer different questions.

Across the 32 repeat-averaged cells, online has lower mean response in 21 (11/16 at B=128 and 10/16 at B=1,024), lower mean queue delay in 19, lower mean service in 27, and lower trial p95 response in 17. Keeping technical repetitions separate gives lower mean response in 38/64 pairs, lower mean service in 48/64, and lower p95 response in 35/64. These counts are not independent workload trials, binomial tests, or evidence of generalization.

Throughput by the fixed nominal horizon is equal in 57 of 64 paired repetitions. Online completes one additional job by the horizon in five pairs and one fewer in two. All jobs eventually complete. The horizon backlog is zero in 22 trials, one job in 105, and two jobs in one; 106 of 128 trials therefore have positive drain. Drain ranges from zero to 15.981 ms. Near-equal horizon throughput does not imply equal service or response latency: both arms face the same arrival count and rate, and most of the finite-horizon difference concerns the final job. Coordinator-server busy fraction ranges from 0.459 to 0.928 across trials and must not be called GPU utilization.

The same 16-job window covers substantially different wall times. Across the four recorded window endpoints per trial, the interval from the first command start to the last completion ranges from 17.216 to 43.933 ms; the corresponding first-to-last scheduled-arrival span ranges from 7.814 to 30.247 ms. For B=128, constant arrivals at ρ=0.50 produce executed spans of 31.931–43.933 ms, while bursts at ρ=0.85 produce spans of 17.216–26.833 ms. For B=1,024, those two conditions give 36.332–43.691 and 21.530–35.387 ms. These spans include intermediate release waiting and service; W=16 is a job-count parameter, not a fixed temporal sampling interval. The terminal window at endpoint 255 is recorded even though the final proposal is suppressed.

Raw timestamp inspection identifies a material timing association. Averaged across all 128 trials, service at job indices 63, 127 and 191 is 9.807, 10.025 and 10.255 ms; terminal job 255 averages 8.292 ms. The other 32,256 job observations average 0.797 ms, although some also have long delays. These indices coincide with native control/observation boundaries and terminal handling. The observations establish an association, not an isolated attribution of the delay to a particular CPU routine, collective, synchronization, or GPU kernel. All of that normal path remains charged. Its periodic cost can affect queues and upper response quantiles even under the always-skip migration policy.

The evidence is limited to two previously observed development document pairs, repeated captured prefill payloads, two technical repetitions, one allocation, one model layer, and this FCFS wrapper. Every arrival is a synthetic release of a preassembled B-token layer invocation. There is no dynamic request batching, full-model request completion, TTFT, decode latency, or production arrival trace here. Numerical and native-route audits establish their declared correctness scope rather than exhaustive proof of every intermediate value. The supplement does not retune τ, ε, W, the dispatcher, or the historical performance criteria.
