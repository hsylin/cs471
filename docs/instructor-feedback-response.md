# Response to the proposal feedback

The feedback identifies weaknesses in the presentation and experimental specification of the original proposal. The original PDF remains a historical proposal; the subsequent studies constitute completed experiments with separate protocols and results. This response distinguishes the initial hypotheses from those results and from work that has not yet been performed. It does not modify a completed protocol, change a final parameter, or reinterpret development measurements as held-out evidence.

The original manuscript mixed scientific exposition with instructions to an implementer. For example, its platform section used the commands “Start,” “Confirm,” and “Keep,” and its evaluation section directed the reader to generate traces and plot measurements. A report of completed work requires descriptions of the actual apparatus, interventions, and observations. In the completed studies, the model revision, GPU allocation, physical replica budget, input construction, and measurement boundary were recorded explicitly. The original hypotheses are therefore reported as hypotheses that were tested, rather than as instructions or established properties of the system. Future tense is appropriate only for an explicitly identified extension.

The typography concern is also substantiated. The first page of the original PDF contains blue hypothesis text, and its controller figure uses coloured boxes and decision labels. This response uses plain Markdown without inline colour directives. Scientific distinctions are expressed through definitions, labels, and prose rather than colour. The existing Markdown result reports do not constitute a revised version of the original PDF. A PDF submission requires a newly typeset, visually checked document with black body text and equations; changing the Markdown prose alone does not change the original PDF or guarantee a monochrome export. The original figure also requires a notation correction, independently of its colours.

Let an engine step denote one execution of the distributed MoE layer on a token batch. Let $B$ be the number of tokens in that batch, $k$ the number of logical experts selected for each token, $E$ the number of logical experts, and $G$ the number of GPU ranks. An observation window contains $W$ engine steps. At an admission opportunity, $t$ labels the current window and $t-1$ the immediately preceding window; these two windows are consecutive and do not overlap. The interval between admission opportunities is $R$ engine steps. Thus the window index is not a token position, elapsed time, or request-arrival index. When $R>2W$, the paired windows used at successive admission opportunities do not form a continuously observed sequence of all intermediate windows.

For logical expert $e$, let $n_{e,t}$ denote its assignment count in window $t$, and let $N_t=\sum_{e=1}^{E}n_{e,t}$ denote the total assignment count. For $N_t>0$, the normalized demand is $p_{e,t}=n_{e,t}/N_t$. The total variation distance between the two window distributions is

$$
D_t=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{e=1}^{E}|p_{e,t}-p_{e,t-1}|.
$$

The statistic $D_t$ lies between zero and one. The stability threshold is $\tau$: the historical stability condition is $D_t\leq\tau$. A low value means that the two normalized histograms are similar. It does not establish constant absolute demand, unchanged within-window ordering, or persistence of that demand after the decision. Native counters in the replicated-input harness contain a common rank multiplicity; normalization removes that common factor, and the recorded physical-to-logical aggregation was verified separately.

Let $M_0$ be the current physical placement and $M_1$ the native planner's proposed placement. For placement $M$, let $m_{eg}(M)$ be the number of resident copies of logical expert $e$ on rank $g$, and $r_e(M)=\sum_g m_{eg}(M)$ its total copy count. Every logical expert remains resident, so $r_e(M)>0$. The equal-sharing approximation to rank load and its peak are

$$
\widehat L_g(M)=\sum_{e=1}^{E}p_{e,t}\frac{m_{eg}(M)}{r_e(M)},
\qquad
\widehat L_{\max}(M)=\max_g\widehat L_g(M).
$$

When the current estimated peak is positive, the predicted relative peak-load reduction is

$$
\Delta_t=\frac{\widehat L_{\max}(M_0)-\widehat L_{\max}(M_1)}
                  {\widehat L_{\max}(M_0)}.
$$

The improvement threshold is $\epsilon>0$. The original stability-plus-improvement rule admits a migration only when both windows are complete, their totals are nonzero, $D_t\leq\tau$, and $\Delta_t\geq\epsilon$; otherwise it retains the current placement. Neither $\Delta_t$ nor $\epsilon$ is a latency reduction or a migration-payback estimate. The improvement-only comparator uses the same current-window estimate and $\epsilon$, without requiring the preceding window or its stability statistic. A cooldown comparator adds a minimum interval $K$, measured in engine steps since the last accepted migration. These definitions precede any method or parameter comparison in a revised exposition.

The first-page native-versus-controller table in the proposal used $D_t$, $\tau$, $\Delta_t$, and $\epsilon$ before their definitions on the second page. Figure 1 additionally replaced these symbols with $D$, $I$, $\tau_{\mathrm{stable}}$, and $\tau_{\mathrm{gain}}$, and displayed strict inequalities instead of the non-strict inequalities in the formal rule. Elsewhere, $I$ denoted assignment imbalance. These are presentation inconsistencies, not alternative implemented policies. The definitions and non-strict inequalities above identify the implemented gate without reusing a gain symbol for imbalance.

The initial amortization hypothesis was that sustained imbalance and larger batches could provide enough execution benefit to offset monitoring and expert-weight movement. The initial stability hypothesis was that comparing adjacent demand distributions could reduce obsolete migrations and improve completed runtime relative to an improvement-only gate while limiting regressions under stable demand. These were distinct claims. A reduction in transferred bytes was insufficient evidence for the second claim, and a gain over unrestricted native migration did not isolate the additional value of the stability statistic.

The parameter history requires a narrower statement than the proposal's instruction to select all parameters on pilot traces. Its worked example used $\tau=0.05$ and $\epsilon=0.10$ explicitly for illustration. The first committed experimental configuration already fixed $\tau=0.10$, $\epsilon=0.02$, $W=16$, and $R=64$. The premeasurement development discussion reduced the illustrative 10% improvement threshold to 2% because examined native candidates could have smaller predicted gains; this was a feasibility choice, not evidence that 2% optimized latency. The archive establishes the implemented values but does not contain a quantitative search over $\tau$, $\epsilon$, or $W$. Consequently, the completed study supports conclusions about this configuration, not an optimal threshold or window length. The values were retained through the corrected pilots and the final gate experiment. The 32-step initial observation prefix supplied two complete windows. [First committed pilot configuration](https://github.com/hsylin/cs471/blob/3ced47c/configs/pilot.json); [frozen final configuration](../configs/frozen/final.json).

The documented parameter search concerned $K$ only. The candidates were 64, 128, and 256 steps, evaluated on pilot seeds 11 and 12. Eligibility required the paired cooldown/improvement-only geometric runtime ratio to be at most 1.02 at each stationary batch size. The score gave equal weight to the seven declared workload variants and both batch sizes. Candidates within 1% of the best score were treated as tied, and the largest tied cooldown was selected. All three were eligible; their all-case ratios were approximately 0.99517, 0.99926, and 0.99903. The declared tie rule therefore selected $K=256$, rather than the numerically fastest pilot score. Earlier incomplete or startup-confounded pilots were retained and superseded by complete corrected attempts; individual slow rows were not deleted. [Pilot selection record](../results/pilot-v3/selection/cooldown-selection.json); [execution history](execution-notes.md).

The original proposal did not specify separate confirmatory hypotheses for the choice of each threshold or window length. Three prospective mechanisms explain why such a study would be necessary. First, a useful stability threshold would need to distinguish ordinary histogram variation from relevant demand changes; that variation depends on batch size, routing distribution, and the number of observed assignments. Second, a predicted-load threshold would need to select gains large enough to matter after the cost of the particular move, which the load proxy does not model. Third, increasing the window length could reduce sampling variation while increasing adaptation delay or hiding periodic changes through aggregation. These are motivations for a future sensitivity study, not newly preregistered claims about the completed measurements. Any such study requires separate development data and a new frozen test. It cannot choose a threshold from the existing final outcomes and present the resulting estimate as held-out confirmation.

The completed gate study directly tested incremental utility and did not establish its prespecified 5% practical gain. On the burst aggregate, the stability gate's runtime ratio was 1.0040 relative to improvement-only admission and 1.0116 relative to the selected cooldown. The stationary comparison passed the 2% regression criterion. Final diagnostic observations also showed batch-dependent stationary variation and phase-dependent histogram aliasing. The stationary medians of $D_t$ were approximately 0.03635 and 0.01260 for batches of 128 and 1,024 tokens; these final observations explain behaviour at the frozen threshold but did not select it. The later identical-state migration experiments separated execution under a preinstalled candidate from the cost of charging its installation. Their findings did not justify implementing another threshold-based controller. [Completed admission study](final-evaluation.md); [completed payback study](payback-final-evaluation.md).

Resident dispatch subsequently addressed a different intervention. It selected among copies that were already resident, using the current batch's logical expert IDs. It neither predicted future demand nor admitted weight migration. The implementation counted current assignments, greedily chose one resident copy for each shared expert under an optimal maximum-active-copy-count constraint, and produced physical expert IDs. The active-copy objective was exact for the frozen residency; token-load balancing was greedy, and no runtime optimum was claimed. The fixed placement, logical top-k routes, combining weights, and native expert kernels remained unchanged. The migration thresholds $\tau$ and $\epsilon$ were not selection parameters of this dispatcher. Native observation and planning remained charged under an always-skip migration policy, preserving an auditable comparison.

The final resident-dispatch result was batch-dependent. Its primary changing-workload ratio was 0.9660, with a 95% block-bootstrap interval of 0.9615 to 0.9701, which did not meet the frozen 0.95 target. The 1,024-token cells improved by approximately 6.70% to 7.29%, whereas the stable 128-token cell averaged 2.93% slower and had an interval spanning the 2% regression margin. These findings do not retrospectively support the initial stability-gate hypothesis. They support a narrower statement about physical dispatch in the tested layer. A batch-dependent production switch was not part of the frozen intervention and was not added after viewing these results. [Completed resident-dispatch study](resident-dispatch-results.md); [final protocol and margins](resident-dispatch-protocol.md); [premeasurement validation amendment](resident-online-validation-protocol.md).

All completed studies used one BF16 Qwen3-30B-A3B MoE layer: layer 0, hidden width 2,048, intermediate width 768, 128 logical experts, top-8 routing, and two H100 94 GB GPUs with NV6 connectivity. The principal comparisons used 32 redundant copies, giving 160 physical slots and 80 slots per rank. Both ranks received the same input batch and combined rank-local expert outputs with an all-reduce. The initial gate experiment used controlled synthetic routes and Gaussian hidden states; the later studies used captured teacher-forced causal-prefill inputs under explicitly constructed replay schedules. The resident study used eight fresh source-document pairs, disjoint from prior documents, and two allocations on the same physical host. These controls establish a one-layer, fixed-resource comparison. They do not establish full-model serving, token all-to-all dispatch, naturally observed temporal demand, or performance on other architectures.

Batch size is not arrival rate. A batch size specifies work per layer invocation; an arrival rate specifies how often externally released work reaches a system. Let $\lambda$ denote released batches per second for a fixed batching contract, and let $a_i$, $b_i$, and $c_i$ denote a batch's release, service-start, and completion timestamps. Its queueing time is $b_i-a_i$, its service time is $c_i-b_i$, and its response time is $c_i-a_i$. The completed back-to-back layer replays did not include such an external arrival process or a request queue. Changes in hotspot identity or in the number of tokens per invocation therefore cannot be reported as arrival-rate sensitivity. A future arrival experiment would require paired release schedules, an explicit queueing and batching rule, rates fixed without using held-out outcomes, and separate reporting of service time, queueing, completed throughput, and any unserved work. Until that extension has been executed, no numerical claim about arrival-rate robustness follows from the existing results.

Architecture sensitivity also remains distinct from workload sensitivity. Varying token batches and expert demand within the same layer does not vary the model's hidden width, expert width, number of experts, routing sparsity, GPU architecture, or interconnect. Kernel-tile variation within this apparatus was an implementation study, not a comparison of model architectures. The observed benefit from reducing activated copies is conditional on these shapes, native kernels, and resident map. A bounded architectural sensitivity extension would require its own defined intervention and correctness scope; it would not automatically represent another pretrained model or production deployment. New arrival or architectural observations can be added as separately identified experiments after their methods and outcomes are available. They cannot alter the frozen thresholds, exclusions, practical margins, or interpretation of the completed studies.
