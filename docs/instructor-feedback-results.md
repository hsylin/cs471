# What the instructor feedback changes

The feedback identifies two substantive gaps in the experiment: an explicit arrival-time model and an architectural explanation of the measured runtime. Both can be addressed within one distributed MoE layer on two H100s with the same fixed replica budget. The writing and notation also need correction, but rewriting them cannot validate a threshold choice or turn a negative experiment into a positive one.

The historical results remain the reference point. The two-window stability gate did not establish useful additional migration-decision information beyond improvement-only admission or the selected cooldown. The later resident dispatcher reduced completed changing-workload layer time by 3.40%, with a 95% block interval of 2.99–3.85%, and therefore missed its frozen 5% target. Its larger-batch results were better, while stable batch 128 averaged 2.93% slower and left the 2% regression guard inconclusive. The new supplement does not change those tests or their outcomes.

## Experimental changes

The old replay submitted layer invocations back to back. Batch size described the work in an invocation; it did not specify an external request-arrival rate. This matters because a service-time improvement may reduce queueing under bursts even when fixed offered load makes completed throughput unchanged. Conversely, an invocation-count window can cover very different wall-clock durations under different arrival schedules. The original proposal did not address either distinction.

The added experiment treats one preassembled batch of 128 or 1,024 captured token records as a layer-service job. It releases 256 jobs against immutable monotonic-clock timestamps, serves them in first-come, first-served order, and finishes every job. Constant releases and simultaneous bursts of eight have the same job count and nominal horizon. Two offered rates, 50% and 85% of a state-specific native capacity estimate, are fixed by the protocol. These are nominal fractions of the synchronized calibration result, not GPU utilization or a known steady-state load. Native-only calibration and all comparisons share one allocation; the online arm cannot determine its own arrival rate. These are synthetic arrivals of captured layer payloads, not measurements of complete language-model requests.

For job $i$, the predetermined arrival is $a_i$, the command start is $s_i$, and acknowledged completion on both GPU ranks is $f_i$. Queueing is $s_i-a_i$, charged service is $f_i-s_i$, and response is $f_i-a_i$. CPU commands, per-job GPU completion synchronization, acknowledgement and delayed wake-up remain in the observations. The report also retains all completions, work left at the nominal horizon, additional drain time, and the duration covered by each native 16-job observation window. Only one layer job is in flight, with no inter-job GPU overlap or dynamic batching. The capacity calibration uses this new synchronized path rather than importing throughput from the older replay. Thus the new response observations are not unchanged historical service times placed under a different arrival calendar.

This is a bounded supplementary check: two existing development source-document pairs, two domains, two batch sizes, four calendars, two arms, and two reversed technical repeats. The 128 trials form 64 paired comparisons and are descriptive; the reused jobs and technical repeats do not supply new independent workloads or a population confidence interval. The prespecified hypotheses concern burst queueing, higher-load amplification of service differences, and whether the smaller batch can amortize dispatch overhead. There is no new controller, rate search or post-result tuning.

The [pinned vLLM serving benchmark](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/vllm-project/vllm/db9527a46873454610df6dbedf79a36d6bf1a7f6/vllm/benchmarks/serve.py) separates arrival rate from request size and latency and supports stochastic interarrival schedules. Our deterministic constant/burst calendars borrow that separation while making the one-layer queue and pairing directly auditable. They do not reproduce vLLM's full serving scheduler or claim equivalence to its burstiness parameter.

The complete run finished on allocation **4871240**, node **kh083**, in **6 minutes 43 seconds**: eight native calibrations and 128 evaluation trials, with all **34,816 jobs** completed. This used the same two-H100 94 GB/NV6 configuration on a different physical host from the earlier final resident study. Independent reconstruction passed for both phases, including calendars, numerical-check attestations, rank completion envelopes, source/assignment hashes, 544 timed native observation windows, fixed maps and zero migration. The 425 CPU tests also passed.

Online dispatch had lower mean service time in **27 of 32** repeat-averaged state/calendar cells, but lower mean response in **21 of 32**. At the individual paired-repeat level, response improved in only **38 of 64** comparisons. Higher offered load increased mean response in all 16 matched state/pattern comparisons for each arm; bursts increased it in 14 of 16 for each arm. These are descriptive counts, not significance tests.

The larger-batch high-load burst case improved on average, while the matching constant-arrival case became worse. Neither can stand alone as the conclusion. Completed-by-horizon throughput was identical in **57 of 64** paired repeats; five favored online and two favored native, each by one completed job. All trials still drained completely. There was positive post-horizon drain in 106 of 128 trials, with a maximum of 15.98 ms. Shorter mean service therefore did not consistently produce lower response, lower tails or more completed work over the scheduled horizon.

Raw per-job timestamps expose a second limitation of a service-average explanation. Jobs 63, 127 and 191, the native planning opportunities, averaged 9.81, 10.03 and 10.25 ms of charged service. The terminal native boundary at job 255 averaged 8.29 ms; all other job positions averaged 0.797 ms. This association implicates the periodic observation/control boundary as a contributor to queueing tails, including when migrations are skipped. It does not isolate native planning, host scheduling, Gloo control, native collectives or GPU work as the unique cause. The terminal policy suppresses its candidate; calling the entire final interval migration time would be incorrect.

The same 16-job observation window covered 17.22–43.93 ms of executed wall time across these trials. A fixed window measured in invocations is therefore not a fixed time horizon. These observations justify explicit arrival, queue, boundary-event and completion records in the report. They do not justify retuning the dispatcher or changing the historical default on these reused development inputs. The original migration-gate experiment remains an invocation-indexed study; the new arrival supplement tests the resident-dispatch service model, rather than retrospectively validating the gate under natural request traffic.

## Architectural interpretation

The architectural reanalysis independently reconstructs physical work from the saved assignment arrays. It explains behavior at one architecture and layer shape; it is not an architecture-sensitivity study over hidden width, expert width, expert count, routing sparsity or hardware. It checked 288 development/final records and deduplicated repeated assignments into 144 state-arm trajectories. Useful two-GEMM work is fixed by the logical routes: $B\times8\times6HI$, with hidden width $H=2048$ and expert intermediate width $I=768$. The resident dispatcher changes how that work is distributed among already present copies, including how many copies execute a padded M tile.

| Batch | Native useful-row fraction | Online useful-row fraction | Native tile-implied work | Online tile-implied work |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 128 | 11.67–12.61% | 14.07–15.26% | 76.7–83.0 GFLOPs | 63.4–68.9 GFLOPs |
| 1,024 | 39.81–40.52% | 47.28–47.79% | 190.8–194.3 GFLOPs | 161.8–163.6 GFLOPs |

These ranges are the four final regime means, each averaged over eight source blocks. Useful arithmetic remains 9.664 and 77.309 GFLOPs per job respectively. The fractions measure useful rows divided by rounded M-tile rows; they are not Tensor Core utilization. Active-weight footprint is likewise not measured DRAM traffic. The [pinned fused-MoE implementation](https://github.com/vllm-project/vllm/blob/db9527a46873454610df6dbedf79a36d6bf1a7f6/vllm/model_executor/layers/fused_moe/fused_moe.py) supplies the tile/kernel interpretation.

The saved development profiles support activation of redundant copies and padding as contributors to expert-kernel cost. A precomputed one-copy assignment had roughly 14–16% shorter two-GEMM residence than a matched precomputed-native adapter. That diagnostic used an offline assignment, not the current online selector. Separate online selector measurements show approximately 13 microseconds of additional selector/launch cost per invocation. Reduced padded work and added dispatch cost together offer a plausible explanation for the observed batch dependence. Neither lower token imbalance nor lower padded work alone proves lower completed runtime.

Hardware counters would distinguish a stronger bandwidth-versus-compute explanation, but they remain unavailable. The separate Nsight Compute probe on allocation 4870934 returned `ERR_NVGPUCTRPERM`; the wrapper recorded the denial and ended without running the expert-counter phase. No driver or permission settings were changed. The [NVIDIA error documentation](https://developer.nvidia.com/ERR_NVGPUCTRPERM) identifies this as restricted counter access. Counter replay can also change execution and cache behavior, so the unprofiled completed timings remain the performance evidence. [NVIDIA profiling guide](https://docs.nvidia.com/nsight-compute/ProfilingGuide/index.html).

## Writing and remaining limits

The new methods use scientific narrative and monochrome figures. The notation response defines normalized expert demand first, then total-variation distance $D_t$, its stability threshold $\tau$, predicted peak-load reduction $\Delta_t$, and its improvement threshold $\epsilon$. It also distinguishes window length $W$ and decision interval $R$, both measured in layer invocations, from elapsed time. Definitions precede comparisons, and the inequalities match the implementation.

The parameter-history correction is substantive. The archive fixes $\tau=0.10$, $\epsilon=0.02$, $W=16$ and $R=64$; it contains no documented optimization grid over the first three. Only cooldown received the declared pilot search and tie rule. The report now describes the former as fixed configuration choices, with plausible sampling-noise/adaptation hypotheses, rather than claiming that their values were empirically optimal. A future sensitivity test would require separate development choices and a new frozen evaluation; it is not necessary to rescue the completed negative result.

The strongest course-project conclusion is consequently narrower and more defensible: this apparatus measures a trade-off between padded expert work, physical-copy selection cost and completed runtime. The arrival supplement examines how that service behavior enters a finite synthetic queue. It does not establish full-model serving speedup, naturally observed request traffic, performance on another architecture, a bandwidth roofline, or external-laboratory replication. The scope remains one layer, one hardware configuration and one fixed replica budget.
